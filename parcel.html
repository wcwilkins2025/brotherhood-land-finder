Soil and water
on one piece of ground

Type an address or draw the parcel's border. You get the government soil survey for that ground, how the NRCS rates it for a house, septic, garden, pasture and pond, the seasonal water table, nearby well depths and the aquifer underneath.

How to use it

  1. 1
    Find the ground. Type a street address, or a town and state, or a ZIP, and press Find. Or just click the spot on the map.
  2. 2
    Draw the border if you have one. Press Draw a border, click each corner of the parcel, then press Finish. Skip this and you get the soil at the one spot you clicked.
  3. 3
    Tick what you want to do with it and press Check this ground.

Where

Soil map unit lines (from the NRCS soil survey) appear when you zoom in close. The photo and topo tiles are from the USGS. Street addresses are placed by the Census Bureau's geocoder, which can be a few hundred feet off; move the pin if it is wrong.

What this can and cannot tell you

The soil survey was mapped at 1:24,000 from pits, borings and air photos, mostly decades ago. A map unit line is good to within a few acres, not a few feet, and every unit has minor soils inside it that are not shown. The ratings are the NRCS's own, computed from the soil's properties, and they describe the typical soil of the unit, not your exact spot. A septic permit still needs a site evaluation or perc test on the ground itself.

The seasonal water table here is the shallow saturated layer the soil scientists found, within the top six and a half feet. It is not the depth you would drill a well to. For that, the nearby USGS wells and the state well-log database give you real well depths and water levels from the neighborhood. Well logs from the neighbors are the best evidence there is.

Nothing here is a legal description, a survey, a flood determination or advice from a licensed anyone. It is a head start on the questions to ask.

Free forever. No login, no tracking, nothing stored about you. Your address goes to the Census Bureau's geocoder to turn it into coordinates. The coordinates go to the USDA soil survey (Soil Data Access) and the USGS water data service to pull the public records for that spot. Nothing goes anywhere else, and nothing is kept.
