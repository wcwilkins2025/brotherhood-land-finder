Land Finder

Every county in the United States, scored for a self-sustaining life on the land. Move the sliders to say what matters to you, then open a county for the facts and the records links.

How to use it

  1. 1
    Type your town or ZIP if you want to stay within driving distance of somewhere. Leave it blank to search the whole country.
  2. 2
    Set the sliders. Each one is Off, Low, Medium or High. Zoning, land price, rain and water are already turned up because they matter to most men.
  3. 3
    Click a county on the map or in the list. The map zooms to it. Keep zooming for aerial photos, then soil lines, then tax parcels: click a parcel for its owner, acres and lines, in the 33 states that publish them free. You get the facts, where each one came from, and buttons to the assessor, deeds, GIS map, health department and listings.

Read this before you sign

A score tells you where to look. It does not tell you what a particular piece of ground is. Do these six things, in this order, on any parcel you are serious about.

  1. 1
    Pull the property card. At the county assessor or appraisal district, look up the parcel. Confirm the acreage, the owner's name, the legal description and whether it says agricultural use. Print it.
  2. 2
    Read the deed and the plat. At the register of deeds or county clerk, find the current deed and any recorded plat or survey. Look for easements, mineral reservations, right-of-way, and whether the seller actually holds title alone.
  3. 3
    Check for taxes owed. The tax collector will tell you if back taxes or liens ride with the land. They become yours at closing if nobody clears them.
  4. 4
    Ask about ag-use rules and rollback. If the land is taxed at an agricultural rate, find out what you must do to keep it (acres, livestock, hay, a lease) and what the rollback tax is if you lose it. In some states the rollback is five years of back taxes plus interest.
  5. 5
    Wells and septic. Run the parcel through the soil and water check first: it shows the seasonal water table, the septic rating and nearby well depths from the government surveys. Then Ask the county health department what a septic permit requires on that soil, and ask neighbors or the state well database how deep the wells run. A perc test before closing is cheap compared to a lot you cannot build on.
  6. 6
    Attorney last, not never. A local real-estate attorney reads the title commitment and the contract for a few hundred dollars. Do it after you have done the first five, so you are paying for judgment and not for homework.
No zoning does not mean no rules. An unzoned county still issues septic permits, enforces state well and floodplain rules, and may have subdivision or road regulations. Call the health department before you assume anything.
Finder's fees are brokerage in most states. Being paid to find land for someone else is licensed real-estate activity almost everywhere. Help a brother for free, or refer him to a licensed agent. Do not take a fee you are not licensed to take.
One deed, several unrelated names, is how men lose land and friends. Going in together feels like brotherhood until one man divorces, dies or needs out. Buy separately, or form an entity with a written operating agreement and a buyout clause before any money moves.
How the score works

Each factor is turned into a 0 to 1 score for every county (for example, land under $2,000 an acre scores 1, land over $10,000 scores 0, in between on a log curve). Your slider sets the weight: Off 0, Low 1, Medium 2, High 3. The county score is the weighted average times 100. A factor with no data for a county is left out of that county's average instead of counting as zero, and the detail panel says so. Nothing is estimated: where a source has no figure, you see "no data".

Zoning is scored three ways. Counties confirmed from the county's own website or an official state list carry a checkmark. Counties in a state where the law settles it (Texas counties cannot zone; Oregon and Washington require it everywhere) carry the statute. Everything else is marked "verify" and scored from the state's general pattern. Always confirm zoning with the county before you buy.
